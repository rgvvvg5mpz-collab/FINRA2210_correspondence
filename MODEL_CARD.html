FINRA 2210 reviewer (Phi-4-mini-instruct + LoRA): model card

What it does. A first-pass compliance reviewer for broker-dealer communications under FINRA Rule 2210. Given one communication plus its distribution metadata, it says what kind of communication it is under 2210, whether it is approvable, which violations it contains (with the verbatim excerpt and rule paragraph), how serious they are, and what the reviewer should do next. It is an assistant to a registered principal, not a replacement: every verdict needs human review.

Input. The communication text (email, letter, SMS, IM, newsletter, social post, web page, ...) plus sender role, firm, recipient type, and distinct retail recipients in the trailing 30 days.

Output. A short review in prose ending with one JSON verdict. The model makes four predictions:

Output Kind Classes
communication_type single-label, 3 classes correspondence: retail recipients, ≤ 25 in 30 days · retail_communication: retail, > 25 in 30 days or public · institutional_communication: institutional investors only
compliant binary true ⇔ no violations and severity none
severity single-label, 4 classes none · low: technical or disclosure gap, little investor harm · medium: misleading content a reviewer would send back · high: promissory, guarantee, projection, fraud-adjacent, or likely Enforcement referral
violations[] multi-label, 0 to 4 per item, 23 categories each entry has category, rule, excerpt, explanation

It also returns required_actions (1 to 3 reviewer steps) and a 2–4 sentence rationale.

Violation categories

How the categories are organized

Every finding the model reports is one of 23 categories. Each category is pinned to exactly one rule citation, so the rule the model writes follows from the category it picks. For reporting, the 23 categories sit in a three-level hierarchy:

  1. Broad group (5). Which part of the rules is broken: misleading content, a missing required disclosure, approval and audience, implied regulator endorsement, or a product-specific rule. Defined as BROAD_GROUPS in taxonomy.py and scored by evaluate.py and the live dashboard.
  2. Sub-group. Descriptive only, and only inside the two large groups. For example, "One-sided" vs "Overclaiming" inside misleading content. Defined as SUBGROUPS in the same file; not scored.
  3. Category (23). The label the model outputs in violations[].category. Defined with its rule and the communication types it can apply to in VIOLATION_CATEGORIES.

Labeling conventions (enforced by the checker on every training item):

  • One entry per category per message, with a verbatim excerpt of at most 200 characters. A message can have up to 4 categories.
  • A category is only used for a communication type it can apply to. The applies-to column below uses C = correspondence, R = retail communication, I = institutional communication.
  • A compliant message has no violations and severity none.

How to read the numbers.

  • Labels: how many items carry the category: in-domain (11,896 items, train + test) / out-of-distribution (OOD, 5,800 items). An item with two categories counts once for each.
  • Test P / R / F1: precision, recall and F1 of the fine-tuned model for that exact category on the 1,186-item held-out test set; test n is how many test items carry it. A dash means the category never appears in the test set or the model never predicts it.

A. Misleading or unbalanced content — 2210(d)(1) · misleading_content

The general content standards. They apply to every communication, including correspondence and institutional communications, and they make up two thirds of all violation labels. The categories overlap by nature (a "can't lose" promise is usually also unbalanced), so the data follows one convention: name the category that best describes each problem, one entry per category, with at most one natural extra.

One-sided

1. not_fair_and_balanced — Not fair and balanced · 2210(d)(1)(A) · C R I

  • Flags: benefits presented without the matching risks, an omitted material fact or qualification, or cherry-picked facts. Also used for a misleading comparison in correspondence or institutional communications, where (d)(2) does not apply.
  • Not this category: the missing item is a product-specific feature (use product_specific_omission); the risk is disclosed but hidden (use buried_disclosure).
  • Example (in_domain-000569, video script, retail): "Our high-yield corporate bond portfolio is paying clients 7.25% a year, deposited monthly." The spot never mentions default, rate, call or liquidity risk.

2. product_specific_omission — Product-specific omission · 2210(d)(1)(A) · C R I

  • Flags: a material feature of the specific product left out: surrender charges on an annuity, the illiquidity of a non-traded REIT or private placement, the daily reset of a leveraged ETF, call risk on a structured note, prepayment risk on a CMO (Rule 2216).
  • Not this category: a general missing risk that is not tied to the product's structure (use not_fair_and_balanced).
  • Example (in_domain-008657, website page, retail): "The correct rate is 5.15% per year, guaranteed for all seven years, with a $25,000 minimum". No surrender schedule and no mention that the guarantee depends on the insurer.
  • Note: rare in-domain (102) but common in OOD (307), because OOD shifts the product.

3. buried_disclosure — Buried disclosure · 2210(d)(1)(C) · C R I

  • Flags: a material risk or qualification that is present but placed in a footnote, legend or fine print in a way that keeps the reader from understanding it.
  • Not this category: the disclosure is missing altogether (use not_fair_and_balanced).
  • Example (in_domain-006627, SMS, correspondence): "*Bonds called by issuer; final redemption at par 6/1/25, no interest paid after that date." The call notice sits after the opt-out boilerplate while the body says payments will continue.

Overclaiming

4. promissory_or_guarantee — Promissory language or guarantee · 2210(d)(1)(B) · C R I

  • Flags: "guaranteed", "can't lose", "risk-free" or "safe" for an investment with market risk, or a promise of an outcome.
  • Not this category: a guarantee that is literally true and properly attributed (an FDIC-insured CD within limits, a fixed-annuity rate backed by the insurer with the claims-paying caveat). These appear as compliant near-misses.
  • Example (in_domain-000471, IM, institutional): "which is guaranteed to hold its value, so there's no risk to the portfolio in the meantime" (about an ultra-short bond fund with a floating NAV).

5. exaggerated_or_unwarranted_claim — Exaggerated or unwarranted claim · 2210(d)(1)(B) · C R I

  • Flags: unsupported superlatives or claims: "best fund", "#1 advisor", "never had a losing year".
  • Not this category: a promise about outcomes or safety (use promissory_or_guarantee); a fund ranking without its required disclosures (use fund_ranking_disclosure).
  • Example (in_domain-004499, email, correspondence): "the Wynfield Equity Income Fund is the single most reliable income fund available anywhere".

6. false_or_misleading_statement — False or misleading statement · 2210(d)(1)(B) · C R I

  • Flags: a materially untrue statement of fact about fees, insurance coverage, product features or credentials.
  • Not this category: each statement is true but the combination misleads (use misleading_in_context).
  • Example (in_domain-003282, mass email, retail): "you can redeem a brokered CD through us at any time at its full face value with no penalty". Brokered CDs are sold on the secondary market, often below face value.

7. misleading_in_context — Misleading in context · 2210(d)(1)(D) · C R I

  • Flags: statements that are individually true but mislead in combination or in the context in which they are made, for example a conclusion that the facts given contradict.
  • Not this category: one statement is false on its own (use false_or_misleading_statement).
  • Example (in_domain-010113, SMS, correspondence): "With 42 consecutive years of payments behind it, this is an income stream you can count on", sent after mentioning a 28% price drop and a 112% payout ratio.

Predicting returns

8. performance_projection — Performance projection · 2210(d)(1)(F) · C R I

  • Flags: predicting or projecting performance, implying past performance will recur, or an unwarranted forecast. Applies to institutional communications too: FINRA's proposed exception for institutional and qualified-purchaser projections is not treated as current rule.
  • Not this category: a clearly labeled hypothetical illustration of mathematical principles (such as compounding at an assumed rate) that does not predict an investment; output of an investment analysis tool under Rule 2214; a price target in a research report with a reasonable basis, valuation method and risks (Rule 2241).
  • Example (in_domain-002129, print ad, retail): "is positioned to deliver rising income and double-digit total returns over the next decade".

9. hypothetical_or_backtest_misuse — Hypothetical or backtest misuse · 2210(d)(1)(F) · C R I

  • Flags: backtested or hypothetical results presented as achievable, or an "illustration" that in substance projects an investment's performance.
  • Not this category: the sender forecasts directly rather than through a backtest or model (use performance_projection).
  • Example (in_domain-009646, newsletter, retail): "That backtest is a good guide to what a ladder built today can deliver over the next decade".

Other people's content

10. third_party_content_adoption — Third-party content adoption · 2210(d)(1), Regulatory Notice 17-18 · C R

  • Flags: forwarding, sharing or linking third-party content (an article, ranking or chart) that the firm has adopted (for example by endorsing it) or become entangled with (for example by helping prepare it), when that content itself breaks the content standards.
  • Not this category: the rep's own words break the standards (use the matching category above).
  • Example (ood-000180, email, correspondence): "found this and honestly it sums it up perfectly. this is exactly why we like these for you!", forwarding a newsletter that calls CMOs completely safe.
  • Note: OOD only (79). The model never saw it in training.

B. Missing required disclosures — 2210(d)(2)–(d)(8), Rule 2212 · required_disclosures

Specific items the rules require. None of them applies to institutional communications; some apply to retail communications only.

Firm identity

11. member_name_missing — Member firm name missing · 2210(d)(3) · C R

  • Flags: a retail communication or correspondence that does not prominently disclose the name of the FINRA member firm (or the name under which it does business). A nonmember affiliate's name may appear if the relationship is clear.
  • Not this category: the member firm is named where a reader would normally look for it, such as the signature block or the footer.
  • Example (in_domain-001781, website page, retail): "Securities offered through a registered broker-dealer, member FINRA (www.finra.org)/SIPC." The page names only the rep's DBA, never the member firm.
  • Note: the model's weakest category on test: it rarely notices the absence and sometimes flags it when the firm is named.

12. brokercheck_link_missing — BrokerCheck link missing · 2210(d)(8) · R

  • Flags: a member's website page meant for retail investors, or a registered person's profile page, with no readily apparent BrokerCheck reference and hyperlink.
  • Not this category: emails, texts and other messages that are not website pages (in-domain it is labeled only on website pages; OOD has one WhatsApp exception).
  • Example (in_domain-011686, website page, retail): a profile page headed "Eleanor Vasquez-Hart, CRPC(R), Registered Representative | Granite Arch Securities, Inc." with no BrokerCheck link anywhere.

Fund performance and rankings

13. fund_performance_disclosure — Fund performance disclosure · 2210(d)(5) · C R

  • Flags: performance of a non-money-market open-end fund shown without standardized 1-, 5- and 10-year (or since-inception) average annual total returns as of the latest calendar quarter-end, the maximum sales charge and the gross expense ratio, or with non-standardized returns more prominent than standardized ones. Applies to correspondence too, so a text quoting a fund's return counts.
  • Not this category: a fund ranking (use fund_ranking_disclosure); a money market fund yield shown with its required disclosures.
  • Example (in_domain-000897, SMS, correspondence): "the Pacific Crest Municipal Income Fund you bought in March is up 5.2% since your purchase".

14. fund_ranking_disclosure — Fund ranking disclosure · Rule 2212 · R

  • Flags: a retail communication that uses an investment company ranking or rating without the ranking entity, the category, the number of funds in it, the period, the criteria, and that past performance is no guarantee.
  • Not this category: a superlative with no ranking behind it (use exaggerated_or_unwarranted_claim).
  • Example (in_domain-004082, mass email, retail): "has been ranked in the top 3% of its category - one of the highest-ranked funds anywhere!"

Recommendations

15. recommendation_disclosure — Recommendation disclosure · 2210(d)(7) · R

  • Flags: a retail communication recommending a specific security (not a fund or variable product) without disclosing market-making, a financial interest, or a recent underwriting role; without offering supporting information; or, for a corporate equity, without the price at the time.
  • Not this category: correspondence, institutional communications, research reports under Rules 2241 and 2242, or recommendations of registered funds and variable products only.
  • Example (in_domain-007240, text blast, retail): "Our pick this week is Cedar Falls (IA) Community School District GO 4.10% due 06/01/2041", sent to 1,270 clients from a firm inventory block with no disclosures.

16. past_recommendations_reference — Past recommendations reference · 2210(d)(7)(C) · C R

  • Flags: a reference to specific past recommendations that were or would have been profitable, without providing or offering a list of all such recommendations for at least the prior year and the required legend.
  • Not this category: the full list and legend are provided or offered.
  • Example (in_domain-003710, website page, retail): "We told clients to buy Corvane Systems at $38 in March 2023. It closed at $112 last Friday."

Claims that need qualifiers

17. unsupported_comparison — Unsupported comparison · 2210(d)(2) · R

  • Flags: a retail communication that compares investments or services without the material differences: objectives, costs, liquidity, safety, guarantees or insurance, fluctuation of principal or return, tax features.
  • Not this category: the same comparison in correspondence or an institutional communication (use not_fair_and_balanced).
  • Example (in_domain-006545, mass email, retail): "Dividend stocks beat money markets on every measure that matters for long-term investors."

18. tax_claim_incomplete — Incomplete tax claim · 2210(d)(4) · C R

  • Flags: "tax-free" or "tax-exempt" without saying which taxes apply (state tax, AMT, capital gains); calling tax-deferred income tax-free; or a tax-deferred vs taxable illustration that breaks (d)(4)(C).
  • Not this category: the income really is free of all applicable taxes, or the claim names which taxes it covers.
  • Example (in_domain-000770, mass email, retail): "Buy them inside your traditional IRA and every dividend and every dollar of gain is tax-free".

19. testimonial_disclosure — Testimonial disclosure · 2210(d)(6) · C R

  • Flags: a testimonial about investment advice or performance without stating that it may not be representative of other clients' experience, that it is no guarantee of future performance, and, if more than $100 was paid, that it is a paid testimonial.
  • Not this category: a client's comment about service (wait times, friendliness) rather than advice or performance.
  • Example (in_domain-009590, mass email, retail): "Jen helped me rebuild my fund lineup after I retired and my portfolio grew 22% last year."

C. Approval and audience — 2210(a)(4), (b)(1), (b)(3) · approval_and_audience

About how a message was handled, not what it says.

20. unapproved_retail_communication — Unapproved retail communication · 2210(b)(1) · R

  • Flags: content that is really a retail communication (a template sent to more than 25 retail investors in 30 days, a seminar deck, a mass email, a social post) that the text or metadata shows was already used without a registered principal's approval.
  • Not this category: material submitted for review before use (that is the normal pre-use posture), or the exceptions in (b)(1) such as interactive forum posts.
  • Example (in_domain-011725, newsletter, retail): "resending this morning's newsletter, which went out to my entire client list at 7:15 a.m." The 207-client send was never approved.

21. institutional_forwarding — Institutional forwarding · 2210(a)(4), (b)(3) · I

  • Flags: a communication treated as institutional although the firm has reason to believe it will reach retail investors: plan participants, an adviser's retail clients, "feel free to share with your clients".
  • Labeling convention: the item keeps the type institutional_communication, and the category records why that treatment fails.
  • Example (in_domain-011585, IM, institutional): "Pass it around to your clients if you want, they'll get a lot out of the duration section."

D. Implied FINRA / SIPC endorsement — 2210(e) · regulator_endorsement

22. finra_endorsement_implied — Implied FINRA endorsement · 2210(e) · C R I

  • Flags: stating or implying that FINRA, another regulator or SIPC endorses, indemnifies, guarantees or approved the firm, its practices or a security: "FINRA-approved", "FINRA-regulated so your money is safe".
  • Not this category: stating FINRA or SIPC membership; "Reviewed by FINRA" or "FINRA Reviewed" on a piece that Advertising Regulation actually reviewed.
  • Example (in_domain-008498, radio script, retail): "our rollover process is FINRA-approved, so you know every step is being done the right way".

E. Product-specific rules — Rule 2220 · product_rules

23. options_communication — Options communication · Rule 2220, 2210(d)(1) · C R

  • Flags: options discussed with retail investors in a way that omits the risks, implies limited risk, or promotes options before the Options Disclosure Document is delivered.
  • Not this category: misleading statements about a non-options product (use the group A category that fits).
  • Example (ood-001245, webinar follow-up, retail): "A conservative, limited-risk way to turn a stagnant portfolio into a dependable income stream" (about covered-call writing).
  • Note: OOD only (16). The model never saw it in training.

Training format. Each item becomes a chat conversation:

  • A fixed reviewer system prompt.
  • A user turn: one of 15 request phrasings, a sentence of context (sender, recipients, 30-day count), and the communication text.
  • The reviewer's answer: the classification with its threshold reasoning, the verdict, each finding as rule + verbatim excerpt + explanation, the rationale, the next steps and the JSON verdict.
  • In 30% of conversations, a follow-up exchange: how to fix it, which finding is most serious, or why it is (or is not) correspondence.

That gives 7,444 single-turn and 3,266 two-turn conversations in train. Loss is computed only on the reviewer's turns, so each two-turn conversation becomes two training examples: 13,271 train / 705 validation.

How the data was curated

  1. Taxonomy first. A working digest of Rule 2210 was written before any data, along with 23 violation categories. Each category is pinned to one rule citation and to the communication types it can apply to; for example, (d)(2)–(d)(7) disclosures apply to correspondence and retail but never to institutional. The digest also defines the difficulty tiers and the severity scale.

  2. Scenarios are sampled before anything is written. Every item starts as a spec, so the data's diversity is set by the specs rather than left to the writer. A spec fixes:

    • difficulty (30/40/30) and the compliance target (~45% compliant);
    • the exact violation categories, weighted toward what review desks see most and limited to those valid for the type and channel;
    • channel, product, sender role, tone and length;
    • the recipient profile and 30-day recipient count, which determine the communication type.

    Compliant medium and hard specs carry a "near-miss" topic. For example: fund returns quoted with full standardized performance, a hypothetical clearly labeled, or "guaranteed" used only where it is literally true. This keeps the model from learning "mentions returns ⇒ violation".

  3. Teacher writing. Claude Code subagents wrote the items, acting as a senior FINRA Advertising Regulation examiner and former chief compliance officer.

    • Claude Opus 5.5 wrote 11,516 in-domain and 5,760 OOD items; Claude Fable 5.1 wrote 380 and 40.
    • Each subagent read the rule digest, the category-to-rule table and the writing and labeling rules, then wrote one item per spec in chunks of 24–40.
    • Each item is the communication in the channel's native format, plus its label.

  4. Automatic validation, repeated until clean. Every chunk was checked against its specs, and the writer fixed and re-checked until it reported zero problems. The checker requires:

    • every rule citation to be the one assigned to its category;
    • every excerpt to be a verbatim substring of the text (≤ 200 characters);
    • no category cited for a communication type it cannot apply to;
    • the communication type to agree with the recipient metadata;
    • compliant, violations and severity to agree with each other;
    • the item to match its spec (difficulty, channel, recipient count, type, categories) and to be 40–6,000 characters long.

  5. Frozen snapshot. Validated output is copied into a timestamped, append-only snapshot with SHA-256 checksums before any cleaning.

  6. Re-validation and de-duplication. Every item was validated again, then de-duplicated:

    • exact duplicates (after normalization) are removed;
    • near-duplicates are removed when more than 50% of their 6-word sequences match an earlier item;
    • OOD items were de-duplicated against in-domain, and the top-up against OOD.

    Result: 0 rejects and 0 duplicates. The largest 6-gram overlap between any two round-1 items is ≈ 0.38, and ≈ 0.24 between the top-up and everything else.

  8. Splitting.

    • Test is 10% of in-domain, stratified by difficulty × communication type × primary category.
    • The split is incremental: when items are added, earlier items keep their train or test side, and only the new items are stratified. A model trained on an earlier train set therefore stays uncontaminated on the current test set.
    • OOD is never trained on.
    • A stratified 5% of train is held out for validation during fine-tuning.

Limits

  • Synthetic labels. These are teacher-model judgments, not FINRA or regulator decisions; have a compliance SME spot-check before relying on them.
  • Chat is a minority of the data. Only 19% of in-domain items are chat: 1,074 SMS, 896 IM and 261 retail text blasts. The rest are email, letters and public retail channels, so chat performance should be measured on its own slice.
  • Two categories never seen in training. options_communication and third_party_content_adoption appear only in the OOD set, so expect low recall on them.
  • Scope. It reviews only the text and metadata it is given. It does not check filing deadlines (2210(c)), recordkeeping or supervisory workflow, or rules outside 2210, 2212 and 2220.
